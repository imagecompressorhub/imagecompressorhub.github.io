How Image Compression Works: Lossy vs Lossless

Every image you save is a compromise between file size and fidelity. Understanding the two families of compression — lossy and lossless — explains almost every choice you will ever make about image formats.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 6 minutes to read

Why images need compressing at all

A digital photograph is, at its core, a grid of pixels, and every pixel needs numbers to describe its colour. A modest 12-megapixel phone photo contains twelve million pixels. Stored completely raw, at three bytes per pixel, that is roughly 36 MB of data for a single picture — before you have taken a second one. Raw storage at that scale would fill phones in a weekend and bring web pages to a crawl.

Compression is the set of techniques that shrinks that data down to something practical: a typical phone photo lands between 2 and 6 MB, and a well-prepared web image can be under 200 KB. The trick is doing it without throwing away anything the eye would actually miss. That is where the two great families of compression come in.

Lossless compression: smaller, but perfectly reversible

Lossless compression reduces file size without discarding a single bit of image information. Decode a losslessly compressed file and you get back the exact pixels you started with, bit for bit. It works by finding and removing redundancy rather than detail.

The classic example is a run of identical pixels. A clear sky might contain a horizontal run of 800 pixels that are all the same shade of blue. Instead of storing that shade 800 times, a lossless encoder stores it once, along with the instruction "repeat 800 times." Real encoders such as the one inside PNG go further: they predict each pixel from its neighbours, store only the (usually tiny) prediction errors, and then compress those errors with general-purpose data compression. It is the same principle as a ZIP archive, tuned for images.

The catch is that photographs contain very little true redundancy — sensor noise alone makes neighbouring pixels differ slightly — so lossless compression of photos typically saves only 10–30%. For screenshots, logos, diagrams and text, where flat colours and sharp edges dominate, lossless is far more effective and, crucially, keeps edges perfectly crisp.

Lossy compression: smaller still, by discarding what you won't miss

Lossy compression takes a different bargain: it permanently throws away information, but chooses information the human visual system is poor at noticing. Done well, a lossy file can be ten times smaller than the lossless version while looking, at a glance, identical.

JPEG, the most famous lossy format, works roughly like this. The image is divided into 8×8 pixel blocks. Each block is converted from "brightness of each pixel" into "which patterns of gradual change are present," using a mathematical transform related to the ones used in audio compression. Human eyes are very sensitive to broad, gradual changes but poor at detecting fine, rapid detail — especially in colour rather than brightness. So the encoder keeps the broad patterns accurately and records the fine detail coarsely, or not at all. The quality setting you choose controls exactly how coarse that recording is.

At high quality settings, what gets discarded is mostly invisible. Push quality too low and the shortcuts become visible as the familiar artefacts of over-compression: blocky squares in skies, smeared edges, and mosquito-like noise around text and logos.

The quality slider, explained

When you move a quality slider from 100 down to 80, you are not removing "20% of the image." You are telling the encoder how aggressively it may approximate fine detail. The relationship is not linear: dropping from 100 to 90 often halves a photo's file size with no visible change, because at 100 the encoder was storing a great deal of detail nobody could see. Dropping from 50 to 30, by contrast, visibly damages most images. This is why the 75–85 range is the working sweet spot for photographs, and why re-compressing an already-compressed image again and again gradually degrades it — each save discards a little more.

Which one should you choose?

  • Photographs for the web or sharing: lossy, usually JPEG or WebP at 75–85 quality. The savings are enormous and the difference is invisible at normal viewing sizes.
  • Logos, icons, screenshots and diagrams: lossless (PNG or lossless WebP). Sharp edges and flat colours are exactly what lossy handles worst and lossless handles best.
  • Images you will edit again later: keep a lossless master copy. Edit from the master, and export a lossy copy for publishing. Never use a published, compressed copy as your new master.
  • Anything with transparency: PNG or WebP, since classic JPEG cannot store an alpha channel at all.

Compression is not the whole story

One final point that surprises many people: resizing usually beats compressing. A 4000-pixel-wide photo displayed in an 800-pixel-wide slot carries five times the data the screen can show. Scaling it down to a sensible size first, then compressing, routinely removes 80–95% of the original file weight. That is why our compressor puts the maximum width and height controls next to the quality slider — used together, they are the difference between a heavy page and a fast one.

Try it yourself: drop a photo into the compressor and move the quality slider from 100 down to 80. Watch the file size fall while the preview stays the same — that is lossy compression discarding detail you were never going to see.

How to Compress Images for a Website Without Losing Quality

"Without losing quality" really means "without losing quality anyone can see." Here is a practical workflow that makes web images dramatically lighter while keeping them visually indistinguishable from the originals.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 7 minutes to read

Start with the real problem: oversized originals

Most heavy web pages are not heavy because of bad compression. They are heavy because the images were never prepared for the web at all. A product photo exported from a camera or a design tool at 4000 pixels wide gets uploaded as-is, then displayed by the browser in a slot perhaps 800 pixels wide. The visitor downloads five times more data than their screen can display, on every visit, on every device. No compression setting can rescue that; the fix has to start with dimensions.

Step 1: Resize to the size the image will actually be shown

Work out the widest the image will ever appear on your site, in pixels, and resize to at most twice that width. The "twice" allows for high-density (Retina-style) screens, which pack two device pixels into each CSS pixel and genuinely do look sharper with the extra resolution. In practice:

  • Full-width hero images: 1600–1920 px wide is plenty for almost every layout.
  • Blog and article images: 1200 px wide covers a generous content column at 2× density.
  • Cards, listings and thumbnails: 600–800 px wide.
  • Logos and icons: export at the display size, or use a vector format such as SVG where the design allows it.

Resizing alone commonly removes 60–80% of a camera photo's weight, before any quality setting is touched.

Step 2: Choose the format that fits the image

Format choice is the second-biggest lever. Photographs belong in JPEG or WebP — WebP typically produces files 25–35% smaller than JPEG at the same visible quality, and every modern browser supports it. Graphics with flat colours, sharp edges or transparency belong in PNG or WebP. The most common mistake we see is a photograph saved as PNG: the same photo that is 250 KB as a JPEG can be 2 MB as a PNG, with no visible benefit whatsoever. Our format comparison guide walks through the decision in detail.

Step 3: Set quality by eye, not by habit

Export at quality 80 and look at the result at 100% zoom, on the kind of screen your visitors use. Check the areas that reveal compression first: gradients such as skies, faces, and any text or sharp logos inside the image. If it looks clean — and at 80 it almost always does for photographs — you can often go lower. Many sites run hero images at 70–75 with no complaints. Avoid the two extremes: quality 100 wastes bytes storing invisible detail, and anything under 55 risks visible blockiness on larger screens.

Step 4: Compare file sizes and check the total page weight

Judge the result by numbers as well as by eye. As working targets, aim for under 200 KB for large images and under 100 KB for ordinary content images, and keep the total image weight of a page under roughly 1 MB. When you compress here on ImageCompressorHub, the before-and-after sizes and percentage saved are shown for every file, so you can see immediately whether a setting change helped or hurt.

Common mistakes that undo good compression

  • Compressing twice. Re-saving an already-compressed JPEG at a lower quality compounds the damage. Always compress from the best original you have.
  • Uploading the "web" image and the original. Some site builders quietly serve the original file anyway. After publishing, load the page and check the actual file size your visitors receive.
  • Screenshots saved as JPEG. Text in screenshots turns fuzzy and noisy under JPEG. Use PNG for screenshots, then resize — they are usually light to begin with.
  • Forgetting the images below the fold. Compress every image on the page, not just the hero. Lazy loading helps perceived speed, but the bytes still transfer eventually.
  • Huge images inside PDFs and documents. If you publish downloads, prepare the images inside them the same way.

A quick routine worth adopting

For every image you publish: resize to at most twice its display width, choose JPEG or WebP for photos and PNG for graphics, compress at quality 75–85, and glance at the result at full size. That four-step habit, applied consistently, is most of what separates fast sites from slow ones — and it takes under a minute per image once it becomes routine.

Put it into practice: the ImageCompressorHub compressor applies exactly this workflow — set a maximum width, pick WebP or JPEG, and use quality 80 as your starting point.

JPG vs PNG vs WebP: Which Format Should You Use?

Three formats cover nearly every image on the web. They are not interchangeable — each was designed for a different job — and picking the right one is the easiest file-size win there is.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 6 minutes to read

The short answer

Photographs: WebP first, JPEG as the universal fallback. Graphics with transparency, screenshots, logos and text: PNG, or WebP where you control the whole pipeline. If you only remember one rule, make it this one — never save a photograph as PNG, and never save text or a logo as JPEG. Most of the benefit comes from avoiding those two mismatches.

JPEG: the photograph standard

JPEG (also written JPG) was designed in the early 1990s specifically for photographs, and its lossy compression is superb at them: smooth gradients, natural textures and millions of colours compress down to remarkably small files. Its weaknesses are equally specific. JPEG has no transparency support, and its block-based compression handles sharp edges badly — text, logos and line art develop fuzzy halos and speckled noise, and get worse with every re-save. JPEG also degrades generationally: each edit-and-save cycle discards a little more, so keep an untouched master of anything important.

PNG: lossless and exact

PNG is a lossless format: what you save is exactly what you get back, pixel for pixel. That makes it the right home for screenshots, interface graphics, diagrams, logos and anything with crisp edges or flat colour, and it supports full transparency, which is why it became the default for overlays and cut-out product shots. The price is size. A photograph contains almost none of the flat, repetitive content PNG compresses well, so a photo saved as PNG is commonly five to ten times larger than the same photo as a JPEG — with zero visible improvement. PNG also ignores quality sliders entirely; the only ways to shrink a PNG are resizing it or reducing its colour palette.

WebP: the modern all-rounder

WebP, developed by Google and now supported by every major browser, was designed to beat both older formats at their own games. Its lossy mode typically produces files 25–35% smaller than JPEG at equivalent visible quality, and its lossless mode is usually around a quarter smaller than PNG. Uniquely, it supports transparency in both modes — a transparent lossy WebP is something neither JPEG nor PNG can offer at all — and it can even store animation. The remaining caveats are about tooling rather than browsers: some older desktop software and a few content platforms still cannot open or accept WebP files, so keep a JPEG copy when you are handing images to other people or older systems.

A decision checklist

  1. Is it a photograph? Use WebP for your own website; JPEG when the file must work absolutely everywhere.
  2. Does it need transparency? Use PNG for maximum compatibility, or WebP for a much smaller file on the web.
  3. Does it contain text, a logo or sharp lines? Use PNG (or lossless WebP). Never JPEG.
  4. Will you edit it again? Archive a PNG or the original camera file as your master, whatever you publish.
  5. Is it animated? Use WebP instead of an animated GIF — the same animation is usually a fraction of the size.

Converting is painless — and often the biggest win

If your folders are full of photographs saved as PNG — a common result of screenshots-of-photos and default export settings — converting them is the single largest saving available to you, often 80–95% per file. In the compressor, add your images, choose JPEG or WebP as the output format, and compare the before-and-after sizes. For graphics that genuinely need to stay PNG, resize them to their display size instead; a 3000-pixel-wide logo shown at 300 pixels is carrying a hundred times the pixels it needs.

One exception to keep in mind: converting a JPEG photograph to PNG never improves it — it only makes the file bigger, because PNG faithfully preserves every artefact already in the JPEG. Convert from the best original you have, in the direction of the web, not backwards.

How to Reduce Photo File Size on Any Device

Email refuses your attachment, a form rejects your upload, your phone storage is full — the fix is the same everywhere. Here is how to make photos smaller on Windows, Mac, Android and iPhone, and in the browser when you would rather not install anything.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 7 minutes to read

First, understand what makes a photo big

Three things decide a photo's file size: its dimensions in pixels, its format, and its compression quality. Modern phones shoot at 12 to 50 megapixels and save at generous quality settings, so a single photo of 4–8 MB is normal. Most of the places photos end up — a profile picture, a marketplace listing, a document, a chat message — display them at a small fraction of that. Reducing file size is mostly about matching the photo to its destination, and you rarely need to sacrifice anything visible to do it.

The quickest route: compress in your browser

On any device with a modern browser, you can skip installing software entirely. Open the ImageCompressorHub compressor, add your photos, and download the results. Because the processing happens on your device, it works the same on a phone as on a laptop, nothing is uploaded, and there are no accounts or watermarks. Two settings do nearly all the work:

  • Quality 80 for general sharing — typically cuts a photo's size by 60–80% with no visible difference.
  • A maximum width matched to the destination: 1600 px for sharing and viewing, 1024 px or less for documents, listings and uploads with size limits.

If a form demands a photo under a specific size — say 200 KB for an application portal — start at quality 80 with a 1600 px cap, check the resulting size shown next to the file, and lower the quality or width a step at a time until you are under the limit.

On iPhone and iPad

The built-in trick is Mail: attach the photo to an email to yourself and iOS offers Small, Medium, Large and Actual Size options — Medium is usually a few hundred KB. For a permanent fix, the Shortcuts app can run a "Convert Image" action that resizes and re-saves photos at a chosen quality, and you can apply it to a whole selection at once. Also check Settings → Camera → Formats: "High Efficiency" (HEIC) photos are smaller on the phone but are rejected by many websites, so converting them to JPEG while compressing solves two problems at once.

On Android

Many gallery apps include an edit option to resize, and Google Photos offers a "Storage saver" mode that keeps slightly compressed copies. For one-off reductions, the browser route above is usually faster than hunting through menus, and it works identically on every Android brand. If you reduce photos often, look for the resize option inside your gallery's editor before reaching for a third-party app — and be cautious with free "photo resizer" apps, many of which exist mainly to show adverts and may upload your pictures to do their work.

On Windows

The Photos app can resize an opened image (look for "Resize image" in the menu), letting you pick dimensions and quality in one step. For batches, the free PowerToys add-on from Microsoft includes an Image Resizer that adds a right-click option to Explorer — select fifty photos, right-click, and produce web-sized copies of all of them. The classic Paint app also resizes by percentage or pixels, though it gives you less control over output quality.

On Mac

Preview, which every Mac already has, covers most needs: open the photo, use Tools → Adjust Size to set new dimensions, then File → Export and move the JPEG quality slider while watching the estimated file size update live at the bottom of the dialog. You can open many images in Preview at once and export them together with the same settings.

Rules that apply on every device

  • Keep your originals. Compression is one-way. Save compressed copies under a new name or in a "web" folder, and keep the camera originals untouched as your masters.
  • Resize before you compress. Halving the dimensions quarters the pixel count — no quality slider can match that.
  • Match the format to the destination. JPEG or WebP for photos; if a site refuses WebP, JPEG is accepted everywhere.
  • Check the result at full size once. Zoom in on a face or a detailed area. If it looks clean there, it will look clean anywhere.
  • Beware of messaging-app "help". Apps that auto-compress photos for sending also strip quality aggressively. When the photo matters, send the file you prepared yourself.

Storage full right now? The biggest wins are usually videos, but photos are second. Compressing a library of 5 MB photos to well-prepared 500 KB copies frees roughly 90% of their space — just keep a backup of the originals somewhere first.

Image SEO: File Size, Names and Alt Text That Rank

Images can bring search traffic in their own right and quietly decide whether the page around them ranks at all. Three habits — light files, descriptive names and honest alt text — cover most of image SEO.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 7 minutes to read

Why images affect rankings twice over

Images influence search in two separate ways. First, they are content: image search is a significant source of visits for recipes, products, travel, tutorials and anything visual, and an image can also appear inside ordinary results, product panels and AI-generated answers. Second, images are weight: they are typically the largest files on a page, and page speed is a ranking input as well as a basic courtesy to visitors. An image strategy that ignores either side — beautiful but enormous, or tiny but meaningless to a search engine — leaves traffic on the table.

File size: fast pages keep their rankings

Search engines measure real loading experience, and the largest element on most pages is an image — very often the hero image that determines the page's Largest Contentful Paint score. Practical targets: hero images under 200 KB, ordinary content images under 100 KB, and a page's total image weight under about 1 MB. Reach them the way our website compression guide describes: resize to display size (at most twice the display width for sharp screens), use WebP or JPEG for photographs, and compress at quality 75–85. Also set explicit width and height attributes on your image tags so the browser can reserve the right space before the file arrives — that prevents the layout jumps that hurt both readers and Core Web Vitals scores.

File names: describe the picture, not the camera

A file name is one of the few explicit labels an image carries, and search engines read it. "IMG_4021.jpg" says nothing; "chocolate-chip-cookies-tray.jpg" says a great deal. Good file names are:

  • Descriptive and specific — name the subject, and the detail that makes this image different from your others.
  • Lowercase, with hyphens between words — spaces and underscores are handled inconsistently, and capitals invite duplicate-URL confusion.
  • Honest — a name stuffed with unrelated popular keywords is spam, and search engines treat it that way. Describe what is actually in the picture.
  • Set before upload — renaming after publishing changes the image's URL and throws away whatever equity it had built up.

Alt text: written for people, read by search engines

Alt text exists first for accessibility: it is what a screen reader speaks aloud and what appears when an image fails to load. Search engines use it because it is usually the most accurate description of an image available to them. Write it as you would describe the photo to someone on the phone — a natural sentence, specific about the subject, usually under about 125 characters.

  • Good: "Golden retriever puppy chewing a stick on a beach" — specific, natural, complete.
  • Weak: "dog" — true, but it wastes the opportunity and helps nobody.
  • Spam: "dog puppy golden retriever puppies dogs for sale best puppies cheap" — keyword stuffing, penalised and useless to a screen-reader user.
  • Decorative images — flourishes, dividers, background textures — should have empty alt text (alt="") so assistive technology skips them rather than reading out noise.
  • Never start with "image of" or "picture of" — the reader and the search engine already know it is an image.

The supporting habits

Captions and surrounding text. The words nearest an image help search engines understand it. A relevant caption and a paragraph that genuinely discusses the picture do more than any hidden field.

One image, one URL. Serve each image from a stable URL on your own domain, and include your important images in your sitemap (or an image sitemap) so they are discovered reliably.

Structured data where it fits. Recipes, products and articles can carry schema that lets images appear in rich results with badges and context — worth adding for content types that support it.

Do not hotlink or steal. Use images you own or are licensed to use. Aside from the legal exposure, an image you do not host can vanish or be replaced at any time.

A pre-publish checklist

  1. Resized to at most twice its display width.
  2. Compressed — under 200 KB for heroes, under 100 KB for content images.
  3. Right format: WebP or JPEG for photos, PNG for graphics and transparency.
  4. File name describes the subject, lowercase with hyphens.
  5. Alt text is a natural, specific sentence — or empty if the image is purely decorative.
  6. Width and height attributes set, so the layout does not jump while loading.

None of this is secret or technical. It is simply treating every image as a small piece of content that deserves the same care as a headline — prepared for readers first, labelled honestly, and light enough never to slow the page down.

Why Large Images Slow Down Your Website

A web page cannot appear until its bytes arrive. Images account for most of those bytes on a typical page, so an unprepared photo quietly taxes every visitor — and mobile visitors pay double.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 6 minutes to read

Every byte has to travel

When someone opens your page, their browser requests the HTML, then works through everything the page needs: stylesheets, scripts, fonts and images. Each file is data that must physically travel across a network before it can be shown. Text is tiny — the words you are reading now weigh a few kilobytes. Images are not. A single unedited phone photo can be 4 MB, which is more than the entire rest of a typical page combined, several times over.

Industry measurements consistently find that images make up roughly half of the average web page's total weight, and on image-led pages — shops, portfolios, recipe and travel sites — the share is far higher. That means when a page feels slow, the odds are strong that images, not code, are the reason.

The maths of a slow connection

Download time is simply file size divided by connection speed, and real-world speeds are much lower than the numbers on broadband adverts. A visitor on a mid-range mobile connection might effectively receive around 1–2 MB per second in good conditions, and far less on a train, in a rural area or on a congested network. At 1 MB per second, a page carrying 6 MB of images needs at least six seconds just for the pictures — before rendering, before scripts, before anything goes wrong. Research on visitor behaviour is blunt about what happens during those seconds: a large share of people simply leave, and they rarely come back to try again.

There is a second, quieter cost. Mobile data is not free everywhere, and a heavy page spends your visitors' money and battery to download detail their screen cannot even display. A 4000-pixel-wide photo shown in a 400-pixel-wide phone viewport delivers ten times the data the display can use.

Big images delay more than themselves

Oversized images do not just arrive late; they hold up everything around them. Browsers give priority to what they judge important, and a huge hero image competes for bandwidth with the fonts and styles the page needs to render at all. If the browser does not know an image's dimensions in advance, the page text loads first and then jumps around as each picture lands and pushes content down the screen — the layout instability measured by Cumulative Layout Shift. And because the largest visible element on most pages is an image, that image's arrival time effectively is the page's loading score, a connection we explore in our guide to image compression and Core Web Vitals.

Why it keeps happening

Almost nobody sets out to publish a 5 MB photo. It happens because modern cameras and design tools export generous files by default, content systems accept whatever is uploaded, and on a fast office connection the result looks fine. The page is only ever tested on the connection of the person who built it. The slowness is real, but it is invisible to the one person who could fix it — which is why checking your actual image weights, rather than trusting how the page feels on your own machine, matters so much.

How to check your own pages

You do not need specialist tools to get a first answer. Open your page in a browser, open the developer tools' network panel, reload, and sort by size: the largest entries will almost certainly be images. Free speed tests such as PageSpeed Insights go further and name the specific images that are oversized for their display slots, along with the savings available. Treat any single content image over about 200 KB, or a page whose images total more than roughly 1 MB, as a candidate for attention.

The fix is preparation, not hope

The remedy is the workflow in our guide to compressing images for a website: resize each image to the size it will actually be displayed (at most twice that, for sharp screens), use an efficient format such as WebP or JPEG for photographs, and compress at a sensible quality. Understand the difference between the two levers and you will reach for the right one — our explainer on resize vs compress breaks it down. A page that sheds 80% of its image weight does not load a little faster; it loads like a different website, especially for the mobile visitors who were struggling most.

Start with your heaviest page: run its images through the compressor at quality 80 with a sensible maximum width, re-upload, and reload the page on your phone with Wi-Fi off. The difference is usually obvious within seconds.

Best Image Sizes for Social Media

Every platform displays images at its own sizes and recompresses whatever you upload. Post at the dimensions each platform actually uses, and your photos stay sharp instead of being mangled twice.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 6 minutes to read

Why uploading the original is a mistake

It feels logical to upload your best, biggest photo and let the platform sort it out. In practice, platforms sort it out aggressively: they resize to their own dimensions and compress to their own quality targets, and their automated pass is rarely kind to fine detail, gradients or text. A photo that leaves your phone at 5 MB may be served to your followers at a tenth of that, with visible softening. You cannot stop platforms recompressing — but you can hand them a file that is already at (or near) the size they will display, compressed carefully by you rather than hurriedly by them. The closer your upload is to the final display size, the less damage their processing does.

For photographs, upload JPEG — every platform accepts it, and its compression behaves predictably. As for file size, there is no prize for uploading 8 MB: once you have resized to the platform's dimensions and saved at quality 80–85, most social images land between 150 KB and 600 KB, upload in a moment, and survive platform processing in far better shape than the giant original did.

A simple routine for every post

  1. Crop to the platform's shape first — decide what the platform will cut off before it does it for you.
  2. Resize to the recommended width (1080 px covers most feeds) using the maximum width setting in the compressor.
  3. Compress at quality 80–85 and keep the original untouched in your library.
  4. Check text at full size. If your image contains words, zoom in: they should be crisp, not fringed with noise.

The same routine doubles as good storage hygiene. Keeping a folder of platform-ready copies means you never scroll through camera originals hunting for the one you posted, and reposting to a second platform takes seconds. Image DPI and Resolution Explained Simply

Few topics in imaging generate more confusion — and more pointless file bloat — than DPI. The good news: for anything shown on a screen, DPI is almost entirely a red herring. Here is what actually matters.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 6 minutes to read

Start with pixels: the only real resolution

A digital image is a grid of pixels, and its true resolution is simply how many: an image that is 3000 pixels wide and 2000 pixels tall contains six million pixels (6 megapixels), however it is labelled. Pixels are the information. Everything else — DPI, PPI, print size in centimetres — is a description of how those pixels might be spread out, not a property of the image itself. Two files with identical pixel dimensions contain identical image data, even if one claims to be 72 DPI and the other 300 DPI.

What DPI actually is

DPI stands for dots per inch, a printing term: how many dots of ink a printer lays down across one inch of paper. Its screen-era cousin PPI (pixels per inch) describes how densely pixels are packed on a display. In an image file, the DPI value is just a small piece of metadata — a suggestion that says "if you print me, spread my pixels at this density." Change that number from 72 to 300 and you have changed nothing about the image: same pixels, same quality, essentially the same file size. Print software reads the suggestion and calculates a default print size from it: 3000 pixels at 300 DPI prints 10 inches wide; the same 3000 pixels at 72 DPI would default to nearly 42 inches wide — printed very coarsely, because the pixels are spread thin.

The 72 vs 300 myth, and why it refuses to die

The myth says web images should be 72 DPI and print images 300 DPI, as if the number itself changed quality. It survives because the underlying truth is real: print needs far more pixels per inch of output than screens do, because paper is viewed closely and shows detail screens cannot. But the requirement is about total pixels relative to printed size, not about the metadata tag. A printer asking for "300 DPI at A4" is really asking for roughly 3500 × 2500 pixels. Send a 3500-pixel-wide image tagged 72 DPI and it prints beautifully; send an 800-pixel-wide image tagged 300 DPI and it prints small or blurry. When a print shop states a DPI requirement, translate it: required pixels = inches × DPI. That is the number to satisfy.

What matters on screens: matching pixels to the display

Screens ignore DPI metadata entirely. A browser displays an image at the size your page specifies, using the pixels available. What matters is the relationship between the image's pixel width and the space it fills: an image shown 800 pixels wide wants about 800 pixels of data — or up to 1600 on high-density "Retina" displays, which genuinely render extra sharpness from the extra pixels. Supply far more than that and you are shipping weight nobody can see, which is exactly the problem our guide to resize vs compress helps you untangle. Supply far fewer and the browser stretches the image, and it looks soft no matter what any metadata claims.

Practical rules you can actually use

  • For the web and social media: think only in pixels. Resize to the display width (times two for retina), ignore DPI completely.
  • For print: work backwards from the physical size. A 6 × 4 inch photo print at 300 DPI needs 1800 × 1200 pixels; a full A4 page needs about 3500 × 2500.
  • When a form demands "300 DPI": check whether it also states a physical size, then supply the matching pixel count. If it truly only checks the tag, any image editor can set the metadata without altering the picture.
  • Scanning documents: here DPI is a genuine setting, because the scanner creates the pixels. 300 DPI is the standard for documents you may print again; 150–200 is fine for screen-only archives.

Notice what is absent from that list: any situation where inflating a small image's DPI or pixel count adds real detail. Upscaling invents pixels by interpolation; it cannot recover information that was never captured. If you need a bigger print than your pixels allow, the honest options are printing smaller, standing further back, or reshooting.

The one-sentence version: pixels are the image; DPI is just a printing instruction attached to it. Manage your pixel dimensions with the compressor's maximum width and height settings, and DPI will take care of itself.

How to Compress Screenshots Without Making Text Blurry

Screenshots are the images most often ruined by compression — and the easiest to keep perfect, once you know why they suffer. It comes down to one mismatch: screenshots are graphics, and most compressors treat them as photographs.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 6 minutes to read

Why screenshots are a special case

A photograph is forgiving. Its content is gradual — skin, sky, foliage — and small inaccuracies blend in unnoticed. A screenshot is the opposite: razor-sharp edges where black text meets a white background, flat areas of exactly one colour, and thin one-pixel lines in windows and tables. Every one of those features is precisely what lossy photographic compression handles worst. JPEG divides the image into blocks and approximates fine detail; around text, that approximation shows up as grey smudges, coloured fringing and a dirty halo that makes words look out of focus. If you have ever seen a support article whose screenshots were barely readable, you have seen this mistake.

Rule one: keep screenshots in PNG

PNG is lossless, which means the text in your screenshot stays bit-for-bit exact — and because screenshots are full of flat colour and repeated patterns, PNG compresses them surprisingly well. A typical full-screen screenshot saved as PNG is often 200 KB to 1 MB, while the same shot as a high-quality JPEG is barely smaller and visibly worse. Our format comparison explains the underlying trade-off, and our guide to how compression works explains why flat, repetitive content is lossless compression's favourite food. The practical takeaway: in the compressor, add your screenshot and keep the output format as PNG — then shrink it with dimensions, not quality, since PNG has no quality setting to lower.

Rule two: capture less, then resize carefully

The biggest screenshot files are full-screen captures of high-resolution displays — a 4K or Retina screen produces an image 3840 pixels wide or more, most of it irrelevant to what you are showing. Two habits fix this:

  • Capture a region, not the screen. Every operating system has a region capture shortcut. Cropping to the relevant window often removes 70% of the pixels before you start.
  • Resize to the display width. A screenshot destined for a help article or document will be shown at perhaps 800–1200 pixels wide. Set that as the maximum width and the file shrinks in proportion — while text at the final size stays sharp, because it was rendered from clean original pixels.

One caution: avoid resizing a screenshot more than once, and avoid odd scaling percentages when the screenshot will be viewed at 100%. Each resample softens edges slightly; a single, deliberate resize from the original is the clean approach.

Rule three: reduce colours before you reduce anything else

Many screenshots use a tiny palette — interface greys, one accent colour, black text — even though the file stores millions of possible colours. Reducing a PNG to an indexed palette of 256 colours (sometimes called PNG-8) can cut its size dramatically with no visible change, because the palette still contains every colour the image actually uses. This works brilliantly for interface shots, diagrams and settings windows. It works badly for screenshots that contain a photograph or a video frame, where the limited palette produces banding — for those, keep the full-colour PNG and rely on resizing.

When a screenshot must be a JPEG anyway

Some systems — certain form uploads, older forums, a few chat tools — accept only JPEG. If you are forced down that road: resize first so the text is large relative to the final image, use the highest quality you can afford (90 or above), and inspect the result at 100% zoom, reading actual words rather than glancing at the whole picture. Text that survives at 100% will survive anywhere. And if the screenshot's whole purpose is to be read — an error message, a settings panel, a receipt — consider whether a smaller crop at full quality serves the reader better than a full screen squeezed harder.

A note on screenshots with transparency

Occasionally a screenshot or interface mock-up carries a transparent background — a captured window with rounded corners, for instance. Transparency rules out JPEG entirely and pushes file sizes up for reasons we cover in our article on transparent backgrounds and PNG size. The short version: keep the PNG, trim away empty transparent margins, and resize to the exact display size, because with transparency those are the only levers you have.

The screenshot checklist: capture a region, keep PNG, resize once to display width, reduce colours if the shot is all interface — and never let a quality slider near your text.

Keep the masters

Marketplaces change their requirements, and shop redesigns change display sizes. If your only copy of a product photo is the 2000-pixel web version, a future requirement for 3000 pixels means reshooting. Keep the original camera files in a separate archive folder, untouched, and treat everything you upload as a disposable copy made for today's purpose. That single habit protects you from almost every future regret in product photography.

The product-photo recipe: shoot or scan once at full quality, archive the original, then export at 2000 px, quality 80–85, JPEG or WebP, on a consistent background — for every product, every time.

AVIF vs WebP: The Next Generation of Image Formats

JPEG has carried the web's photographs for three decades, but two newer formats now beat it comfortably. WebP is the established challenger; AVIF is the more ambitious one. Here is how they compare, and which deserves your images today.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 7 minutes to read

Where things stand

Our comparison of the classic formats ended with WebP as the modern all-rounder: roughly 25–35% smaller than JPEG for photographs, with transparency and animation in the same format, supported by every major browser. AVIF, which arrived later, pushes the same idea further. It is built on the AV1 video codec — technology designed for streaming film — repurposed for still images. That heritage gives AVIF compression tools JPEG's designers never had, and the results show it.

File size and quality: AVIF wins, usually

Head-to-head on photographs, AVIF typically produces files 20–30% smaller than WebP at equivalent visible quality, and around half the size of a comparable JPEG. Its advantage is largest exactly where older formats struggle: smooth gradients such as skies and studio backdrops, where AVIF avoids the banding and blockiness that creep into JPEG and, to a lesser extent, WebP. It also supports a wider colour range and high dynamic range content, which matters increasingly as HDR displays spread.

The gap narrows — and occasionally reverses — on images full of fine texture, and at very high quality settings where both formats are already nearly transparent. AVIF is not magic; it is a better set of trade-offs, most visible at the small file sizes the web cares about.

The catches: speed and sharpness of detail

AVIF's sophistication costs computation. Encoding an AVIF image is noticeably slower than encoding WebP or JPEG — on large batches, dramatically so — and decoding demands more from the visitor's device, though modern phones and computers handle it without complaint. There is also a character difference: at aggressive compression levels AVIF tends to smooth fine texture (skin pores, fabric weave, film grain) into a clean, slightly polished look, while WebP keeps a bit more grit at the price of more artefacts. Photographers who love grain sometimes prefer WebP's failure mode; everyone else rarely notices either.

Support: closer than you might expect

WebP is the safer pair of hands. Every modern browser displays it, most content platforms accept it, and image tools have supported it for years. AVIF's browser support has caught up remarkably — current Chrome, Firefox, Safari and Edge all display AVIF — but the wider ecosystem lags: some social platforms, older apps, email clients and content systems still reject or mishandle AVIF uploads. The pattern is the one we saw with WebP a few years earlier: browsers first, everyone else eventually.

Which should you choose?

  • Your own website, performance-critical: AVIF for photographs, with WebP or JPEG as the fallback for older browsers. Serving AVIF where supported is one of the largest remaining image optimisations — it directly lightens the hero images that decide your Core Web Vitals.
  • Maximum compatibility — files you hand to other people or platforms: WebP for the web, JPEG when the destination is unknown or old. Nobody has ever been unable to open a JPEG.
  • Graphics with transparency or animation: WebP remains the practical choice today; it handles both in one mature format.
  • Archiving: neither. Keep your masters in their original form (or a lossless format); delivery formats are for publishing, not for keeping.

A note on this site: the ImageCompressorHub compressor works with JPEG, PNG and WebP because those are the formats browsers can both read and write natively — AVIF encoding in the browser is still too slow and unevenly supported to offer honestly. If your publishing platform converts uploads to AVIF for you, that is the best of both worlds: prepare a clean, well-sized WebP or JPEG, and let the platform's servers do the heavy encoding.

The direction of travel

Formats evolve slowly and then suddenly. JPEG will remain openable forever, WebP is the web's current workhorse, and AVIF is steadily taking over the high end wherever delivery is automated. The practical lesson is the same one running through all our guides: keep untouched masters, and treat the published format as a replaceable decision you can revisit whenever the landscape shifts — because it will.

Bottom line: AVIF is smaller, WebP is safer, JPEG is universal. Choose per destination, keep your originals, and never let format debates delay the simpler wins — resizing and sensible quality.

Image Metadata (EXIF): What Your Photos Reveal and How to Remove It

Every photo you take carries a hidden passenger: a block of data recording where you were, when you were there, and what you were holding. Most of the time it is harmless. Sometimes it tells strangers far more than the picture does.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 7 minutes to read

What EXIF data is

EXIF — Exchangeable Image File Format — is the standard way cameras and phones embed information inside a JPEG or TIFF file. A typical photo's EXIF block includes the date and time, the camera or phone model, lens and exposure settings (aperture, shutter speed, ISO), whether the flash fired, the software version that processed the shot — and, if location services were on, the GPS coordinates of the exact spot where you were standing, accurate to a few metres. Related standards add more: IPTC fields for captions, credits and copyright, and XMP data recording edits made in photo software. None of this is visible when you look at the picture. All of it travels with the file wherever the file goes.

Why it deserves your attention

The GPS field is the headline risk. Post a photo of an item you are selling, taken in your living room, on a site that does not strip metadata, and you may have published your home address to anyone who downloads the image and inspects it. Photos of children at home or school, of a car in its usual parking place, of anything valuable where it is kept — all can carry the same quiet disclosure. Timestamps add a second layer: a set of photos can reveal a household's routine, or prove a home is empty during a holiday the owner is posting about in real time.

Context matters, and panic is not warranted. Major social platforms strip EXIF data from uploaded photos as a matter of course, and for most casual sharing the practical risk is low. The risk concentrates in original files shared directly: marketplace listings, email attachments, file downloads, forums and smaller platforms that serve your file untouched. Those are exactly the places where a moment's preparation pays.

How to see what your photos are carrying

On Windows, right-click a photo, open Properties and the Details tab — you will find camera data and, if present, GPS coordinates. On a Mac, Preview's inspector (Tools → Show Inspector, the info tab) shows the same. Phones hide most of it: iOS Photos shows location on a map when it exists, which is itself a useful check. If the location is recorded and you did not intend it, assume the rest of the block is there too.

How to remove it

  • Compress or convert the photo. When an image is redrawn by a tool like our compressor, the new file is written from pixels alone — EXIF data, including GPS, is simply not carried across. Shrinking a photo for sharing therefore strips its metadata as a side effect, a point we note in our guide to reducing photo file size.
  • Use the built-in remove option. Windows' Properties dialog offers "Remove Properties and Personal Information" at the bottom of the Details tab, which writes a cleaned copy.
  • Turn off location tagging at the source. In your camera or phone settings you can stop GPS being recorded in the first place — the cleanest fix for future photos, though it does nothing for your existing library.
  • Be careful with dedicated "EXIF remover" apps. The irony of uploading a photo to a random service to protect your privacy should be obvious. Prefer tools, like this one, that work locally on your device.

When to keep metadata instead

EXIF is not purely a liability. Photographers use it to remember settings and improve; families use dates and locations to organise decades of memories; copyright and credit fields assert ownership. The sensible practice is a split one: keep your original files, metadata and all, in your private archive — and share cleaned copies. Compression gives you that split naturally, since you should be keeping originals and sharing prepared copies anyway. Strip for the audience, keep for yourself, and your photos will tell the story you chose — and only that story.

Quick check before your next listing or post: if you are sharing an original camera file anywhere that serves it untouched, run it through the compressor first. You get a smaller file and a metadata-free one in the same step.

The result is not just faster; it is more consistent, which matters when the photos will be seen together, as products in a shop or pictures in one album.

Step 1: Pick the recipe from the destination

  • Web galleries and blogs: maximum width 1600 px, JPEG or WebP, quality 80.
  • Product photos for a shop: longest side 2000 px, quality 82–85 — see our product photo guide for the reasoning.
  • Email and messaging: maximum width 1920 px, quality 80, per our email size guide.
  • Upload forms with a stated limit: work backwards from the limit — start at 1600 px and quality 80, then step down until the largest file in the batch fits.

Notice that every recipe pairs a dimension with a quality. The dimension does most of the work — as our resize vs compress explainer shows, halving dimensions quarters the pixel count — and the quality setting finishes the job.

Step 2: Test on the hardest photos first

Before running all two hundred, pick three to five photos that stress the recipe: one with fine detail (foliage, fabric, hair), one with smooth gradients (sky, studio background), one taken in low light, where noise inflates file sizes and hides artefacts. Two organisational rules make the output usable. First, never let compressed files land in the same folder as the originals under the same names — keep a clearly named output folder ("web", "email-ready", "shop-2000px") so future-you can tell masters from copies at a glance. Second, keep the originals, full stop: compression is one-way, storage is cheap, and the batch you make for today's destination will not suit tomorrow's.

Step 4: Spot-check the results by size, then by eye

After the run, sort the output folder by file size. Outliers are information: a file that is three times larger than its neighbours usually contains noise, fine texture or a near-duplicate of a photo that compresses poorly — glance at it to confirm it looks right. A file that is suspiciously tiny may have been a small image to begin with, enlarged by nothing and compressed to mush — check it too. Then open two or three at random and view them at the size they will actually be seen. Batch work fails silently or not at all; a two-minute spot-check is what makes it trustworthy.

When not to batch

Mixed sets deserve a split. A folder containing both photographs and screenshots should be separated first — the screenshots want PNG and resizing only, as our screenshot guide explains, while the photos want JPEG or WebP at quality 80. Likewise, a handful of hero images destined for a homepage deserve individual attention at higher quality. Batch the many, hand-finish the few: that division of labour is the whole secret.

The batch habit in one line: one destination, one recipe, tested on the hardest photos, applied to all, spot-checked after — and the originals never touched.

Fixing 'File Too Large' Upload Errors

Job applications, visa portals, university forms, marketplaces — sooner or later a website refuses your photo. The rejection feels arbitrary, but it follows rules, and once you know them the fix takes a minute.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 6 minutes to read

Read the error properly: three different limits wear one disguise

"File too large" can mean three separate things, and each has a different cure:

  • File size — the file's weight in KB or MB exceeds the cap ("maximum 200 KB"). Cured by compressing and resizing.
  • Dimensions — the image's pixel width or height is outside an allowed range. Some portals want a passport-style photo of an exact size, such as 600 × 600 px; others reject images above a pixel ceiling. Cured by resizing to the stated dimensions.
  • Format — the site accepts only certain types, usually JPEG or PNG, and reports anything else confusingly. A photo in HEIC (iPhone's default) or WebP may be rejected even at a tiny size. Cured by converting to JPEG.

Read the form's instructions — the small print near the upload button — before touching the file. Knowing whether you are chasing 200 KB, 600 × 600 pixels, or a JPEG usually turns a frustrating loop of guesses into a single successful attempt.

The fix, in the order that works

1. Convert to the accepted format first. If the photo is HEIC, PNG or WebP and the form wants JPEG, convert it: add it to the compressor and choose JPEG as the output format. A photograph converted from PNG to JPEG often shrinks by 80% in this step alone — the format mismatch our format guide warns about is the single most common cause of stubborn uploads.

2. Resize to the destination. If the form states pixel dimensions, match them. If it does not, resize to a sensible ceiling for the purpose — 1600 px wide for a document or listing photo, 1024 px or less for a profile or ID-style image. Resizing is the most powerful shrink available; our resize vs compress explainer shows why it beats quality adjustments.

3. Lower quality in steps. Start at 80 and check the resulting file size, which the compressor shows next to each file. Still too big? Try 70, then 60, and if the limit is truly strict — some portals demand under 50 KB — reduce the dimensions further as well. Small display sizes forgive low quality: an ID photo shown at 300 pixels wide looks fine at settings that would ruin a poster.

4. Check the actual number before uploading. Compare the final file size with the limit, leaving a small margin. If the form counts in KB, remember that 200 KB on the form may mean 200,000 bytes or 204,800 depending on the system — aim a little under.

Scenario notes

Job and university applications usually cap a photo or scanned document at 200 KB–2 MB. Scans are the trap: a scanner set to 600 DPI produces enormous files. Rescan at 200–300 DPI if you can — our DPI explainer covers why that loses nothing readable — or compress the existing scan as above.

Government and visa portals are the strictest, often combining a small KB cap with exact pixel dimensions and a plain background requirement. Meet the dimensions exactly, then adjust quality until the size fits; do not crop or stretch the photo to fit, as that can breach the photo rules themselves.

Marketplaces and classifieds rarely reject for size alone, but very large uploads time out on slow connections and get compressed harshly when they succeed. Preparing the photo yourself, as in our product photo guide, gives a faster upload and a better-looking listing.

If it still will not upload

Work through the unglamorous causes: the file name containing unusual characters (rename it to something plain like photo.jpg), a second hidden file being included, the browser caching an earlier failed attempt (refresh and retry), or the site's own limit being lower than its instructions claim (aim for half the stated cap and test). And keep perspective: the file you are fighting with is a copy. Your original stays untouched on your device, so you can afford to compress the copy as hard as the form demands — our guide to reducing photo file size has gentler settings for occasions when quality matters more than the cap.

The universal recipe: JPEG format, dimensions matched to the form (or 1600 px wide at most), quality stepped down from 80 until the size fits — that combination satisfies nearly every upload limit on the internet.

Image Compression and Core Web Vitals: Passing the LCP Test

Google measures how your pages feel to real visitors, and the single biggest factor is usually an image. If your Largest Contentful Paint is failing, your hero image is the first place to look — and the easiest to fix.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 7 minutes to read

What Core Web Vitals are, briefly

Core Web Vitals are three measurements Google uses to judge page experience, and they feed into search rankings. Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) times how long the main content — usually the biggest image or headline block — takes to appear; the target is 2.5 seconds or less. Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS) measures how much the page jumps around while loading; the target is a score under 0.1. Interaction to Next Paint (INP) measures responsiveness when a visitor taps or clicks; images play only a minor role there. For most sites, images decide the first two scores outright — which is good news, because images are the easiest part of a page to fix without a developer.

Why your LCP is almost certainly an image

The "largest contentful" element is, by definition, the biggest thing in the first screenful. On homepages, product pages, articles and landing pages, that is overwhelmingly the hero image or the main product photo — text blocks rarely out-render a photograph. So the LCP clock is, in practice, timing how long your main image takes to download and display. Every kilobyte you remove from that image shortens the score almost directly. This is the sharpest version of the general problem we describe in why large images slow down your website: not just "the page is heavy," but "this specific file is the metric."

You can confirm it in seconds: run your page through PageSpeed Insights and it will name the LCP element explicitly. When it names an image — it usually does — you have your assignment.

The four fixes that move LCP

  1. Compress the hero image properly. Resize it to its display size (at most twice as wide, for retina screens) and save at quality 75–85. A hero that arrives at 1.8 MB and leaves at 180 KB is a common, entirely realistic outcome of the workflow in our website compression guide.
  2. Use a modern format. WebP cuts roughly a quarter to a third from a JPEG's weight; AVIF can halve it. Our AVIF vs WebP comparison covers when each is appropriate. If your platform serves modern formats automatically, make sure the feature is on.
  3. Do not lazy-load the hero. Lazy loading is right for images below the fold, but applied to the LCP image it delays the very thing being timed. The main image should load eagerly and early; everything else can wait.
  4. Keep the total page in proportion. A light hero cannot rescue a page carrying forty heavy gallery images. Compress everything, with the hero getting the most care.

CLS: the image problem with a two-minute fix

Layout shift happens when the browser lays out text, then an image arrives and shoves everything down to make room. The cure is not compression but information: include width and height attributes (or an equivalent aspect-ratio style) on every image tag, so the browser reserves the correct space before the file downloads. The text never moves, the shift score collapses, and readers stop losing their place mid-sentence. It is the cheapest Core Web Vitals win available, and it is also one of the checklist items in our image SEO guide, because the same habits serve both rankings systems.

Working targets and honest expectations

Aim for a hero image under 200 KB, ordinary content images under 100 KB, and — if you serve AVIF or WebP — lower still. Then retest. One caution about scores: lab tests (including PageSpeed's default simulation) model a mid-range phone on a throttled connection, so they are stricter than your own browsing experience — deliberately. Passing under those conditions means real visitors, on real networks, get a page that feels instant. Chasing a perfect 100 is rarely worth it; moving a failing LCP of 5 seconds to a passing 2 is worth a great deal, and images alone usually get you there.

Your next ten minutes: find your LCP image in PageSpeed Insights, run it through the compressor at its display size and quality 80, re-upload, and retest. For most sites, that single file is the difference between failing and passing.

Transparent Backgrounds: Why PNG Files Get So Big and What to Do

You cut out a product, saved it with a transparent background, and the file came out at 4 MB. Nothing went wrong — transparency is genuinely expensive. Here is why, and the ways around it.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 6 minutes to read

Transparency means an extra channel of data

A normal image stores three numbers per pixel: red, green and blue. A transparent image stores a fourth — the alpha channel — recording how see-through each pixel is, from fully opaque to fully invisible. That is a third more data before compression even starts. Worse, the edges of a cut-out are the hardest content to compress: a product's outline against nothing is a long, detailed, high-contrast border, and semi-transparent pixels around hair, fur, glass or soft shadows are all unique values that resist pattern-matching. The alpha channel also defeats many of the tricks lossy formats use, because a slightly wrong alpha value shows as a visible halo or fringe.

And transparency rules out the small formats

The deeper problem is format lock-in. JPEG — the web's efficient photo format — has no alpha channel at all, so a transparent image simply cannot be a JPEG. That leaves PNG as the default, and PNG is lossless: it stores every pixel exactly, which is wonderful for a logo and punishing for a photograph, as our format comparison details. A transparent product photo therefore pays three times: the extra channel, the difficult edges, and a format that cannot discard anything. A 4 MB result for a large cut-out is not a mistake; it is arithmetic.

First question: do you actually need transparency?

Many transparent images are placed, in the end, on a plain white or solid-coloured page — product grids, presentation slides, documents. If the final background is known and fixed, flatten the image onto that background and save as JPEG or WebP. The file collapses to normal photo size, because it is now a normal photo. Keep the transparent master in your archive for the day a different background is needed. This one decision — flatten when the destination is known — eliminates most oversized PNGs in the wild.

When you do need it: the alternatives

  • WebP with transparency. WebP supports an alpha channel in both its lossy and lossless modes. A transparent lossy WebP is typically a quarter to a tenth the size of the equivalent PNG, and every modern browser displays it. For transparent images on your own website, this is usually the answer — add the PNG to the compressor and choose WebP as the output format to see the difference on your own file.
  • AVIF with transparency. AVIF also supports alpha and compresses even harder; our format comparison covers its support trade-offs.
  • SVG for graphics. If the "image" is really a logo, icon or illustration drawn from shapes, a vector SVG is tiny, perfectly sharp at any size, and transparent by nature. This applies to drawn graphics, not photographs.
  • A smaller, palette-reduced PNG. If PNG is mandatory — a marketplace that accepts nothing else, say — resize to the exact display size and, for simple graphics, reduce the colour palette. Every pixel you trim is a pixel PNG no longer has to store exactly.

Trim the emptiness

Cut-outs often carry a generous margin of fully transparent pixels around the subject — leftover canvas from the editing session. Those pixels are cheap individually (long identical runs compress well) but they inflate the dimensions, and dimensions drive everything else, including how large the image is treated by whatever displays it. Crop tightly to the subject, resize to the largest size it will genuinely be shown — for shop images, the standards in our product photo guide apply — and only then export. A tightly cropped 1200-pixel cut-out in WebP will outperform a sprawling 4000-pixel PNG by an order of magnitude, while looking identical on the page.

The transparency rule: if the background is known, flatten and use JPEG or WebP; if it is not, keep the master PNG but publish a transparent WebP — and never ship a photo-sized PNG to a web page by default.

How Much Quality Loss Is Acceptable? The 80–85% Quality Rule Explained

Somewhere along the way, "save photos at quality 80" became the web's folk wisdom. It is good advice — but it is widely misunderstood, and knowing why it works tells you exactly when to break it.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 6 minutes to read

First, unlearn what the number means

Quality 80 does not mean "80% of the quality" or "20% of the image removed." The quality scale is an instruction to the encoder about how precisely to record fine detail, and it is neither linear nor standardised: one program's 80 is not identical to another's, because each encoder implements the scale its own way. As our guide to how compression works explains, the top of the scale is where the surprise lives — moving from 100 to 90 commonly halves a photo's file size with no visible change at all, because quality 100 spends enormous bytes recording detail finer than eyes can detect. The interesting decisions all happen between 50 and 95, and the file size falls steeply at the top of that range and flattens at the bottom.

Why 80–85 became the rule

The 80–85 band earned its reputation by sitting at the knee of that curve. Above it, you pay many kilobytes for improvements nobody can see outside a pixel-by-pixel comparison. Below it, savings shrink while artefacts — blockiness in gradients, smearing around edges, noise near text — start becoming visible at normal viewing sizes. At 80–85, a photograph displayed at its intended size is, for nearly all viewers on nearly all screens, indistinguishable from the original, at a third to a fifth of the weight. That combination — invisible difference, dramatic saving — is exactly what a rule of thumb is for.

When to go higher

  • Images people will zoom into: product photos, portfolio pieces, anything sold on its detail. Use 85–92 and accept the larger file; scrutiny is the use case quality exists for. Our product photo guide works in this range.
  • Images that will be edited or re-saved: every lossy save discards a little more, so a file destined for further work should start high — or better, stay lossless until its final export.
  • Subtle gradients: fog, dusk skies, studio backdrops band visibly at lower settings. Give them a few extra points.

When to go lower

  • Small display sizes: a thumbnail or avatar shown at 200 pixels wide hides artefacts completely; 60–70 is plenty, and the file becomes tiny.
  • Background and decorative images: anything blurred, darkened or sitting behind text is judged by nobody. Compress boldly.
  • Hard limits: when a form demands 100 KB, the limit outranks aesthetics — step down until it fits, as our upload errors guide describes. At small display sizes, the result still looks fine.

Judge with your eyes, at the real size

The only reliable test is looking. Export at your candidate setting, view the image at the size it will actually be displayed — not zoomed to 400%, where every setting looks terrible and the comparison stops meaning anything — and look at the revealing places: faces, edges against sky, fine texture, any text. Then, if you want to be thorough, view at 100% as well; artefacts invisible at display size but faintly present at 100% are a reasonable trade for a much smaller file, because your audience will never see the 100% view. Flip between original and compressed quickly: if you cannot reliably tell which is which, the setting is right, whatever the number says.

The rule behind the rule

Acceptable loss is not a property of the image; it is a property of the viewing. A billboard, a phone screen, a printed page and a 100-pixel avatar all forgive different amounts. So treat 80–85 as the starting point it is: the default for ordinary photographs at ordinary sizes, adjusted up for scrutiny and down for smallness — and always, always applied to a copy, with the untouched original kept safe. Loss you chose, at a level you tested, is a tool. Loss that happened to you by accident is just damage.

Try the test yourself: compress one photo in the compressor at 95, 85, 75 and 60, and compare the sizes against what your eyes can actually detect. Most people find their personal sweet spot within a point or two of 80 — and the file sizes usually shock them.

Why GIFs Are So Heavy — and the Smaller Alternatives

A ten-second GIF can be larger than the full video clip it was cut from. That is not an exaggeration but a consequence of 1987 technology doing a 2026 job. Here is why GIFs balloon, and what to use instead.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 6 minutes to read

A format from another era

GIF — Graphics Interchange Format — was introduced in 1987, when a fast connection moved data slower than a modern phone receives in a blink, and its animation support was a modest extension of a still-image format. Nothing about it was designed for moving pictures of the photographic kind. Three structural limits explain its weight:

  • 256 colours, total. Every frame is squeezed into a palette of at most 256 colours. Photographic content — a face, a sunset, a football match — contains millions, so the encoder dithers, scattering dots to fake the missing shades, which adds noise that itself needs storing.
  • Frame-by-frame thinking. A GIF is essentially a stack of still images. It can skip redrawing pixels that do not change between frames, but it has none of the motion prediction that video codecs use to describe movement compactly.
  • No modern compression. Its lossless compression predates the techniques in PNG, let alone WebP or video codecs — the same family of limitations we discuss in how compression works.

The result: a five-second, modest-resolution GIF of real-world footage commonly lands between 5 and 20 MB. The same clip as a proper video file is often under 1 MB — better looking, with sound available, at a tenth of the weight.

The best alternative is usually a video file

If the animation is photographic — screen recordings, product demos, clips, reactions — the right container is a video: MP4 (H.264) for universal compatibility, or WebM for even smaller files on the web. Video codecs were built for exactly this: they track how blocks of pixels move between frames and store the movement, not a fresh picture each time. Every modern platform that appears to show "GIFs" — social feeds, messaging apps, image hosts — quietly converts uploads to video for precisely this reason. When a site lets you embed an MP4 with autoplay, muted and looping attributes, visitors see a "GIF" that loads ten times faster and plays more smoothly. That is not a compromise; it is what the big platforms already do behind the curtain.

When you need an image format: animated WebP and AVIF

Sometimes a genuine image file is required — an email client, a forum, a documentation page that will not embed video. Animated WebP is the direct modern successor: it supports full colour, transparency and far better compression, typically producing files 60–80% smaller than the equivalent GIF at visibly better quality. Animated AVIF goes further still on size, with the support caveats described in our AVIF vs WebP comparison. Either one ends the 256-colour era for your animations.

If you must ship a GIF, shrink it properly

  • Reduce the dimensions. Most GIFs are displayed small; a 480-pixel-wide version carries a fraction of the data of a 1080-pixel one.
  • Trim the length and frame rate. Halving the frames halves the file. Many animations read perfectly at 12–15 frames per second instead of 24–30.
  • Crop tightly. Cut everything the viewer does not need to watch; static regions still cost data in GIF.
  • Limit colours deliberately. Flat, graphic content — a loading spinner, a simple diagram — genuinely suits a small palette and compresses beautifully. Save GIF for that kind of content, where it was always at home.

Where GIF still earns its place

GIF survives because it works everywhere, including ancient software and stubborn email clients, and for tiny, simple, flat-colour animations — a blinking cursor graphic, a three-frame icon — its overhead is trivial and its compatibility unmatched. The mistake is not using GIF; it is using it for photographic motion, where it is the heaviest possible choice. Match the format to the content, the same principle behind every decision in our format guide, and your pages, chats and documents all get lighter at once.

A revealing comparison: take a short clip you would normally GIF-ify and check the file sizes — the video will usually be smaller than the still photos in our compressor queue. Animation is video's job now; let it do it.

Resize vs Compress: What Is the Difference and Which Do You Need?

People use "resize" and "compress" as if they were the same button. They do different things, to different properties of the image — and choosing the right one, in the right order, is most of practical image optimisation.

ImageCompressorHub Guides · About 6 minutes to read

The two operations, defined plainly

Resizing changes the image's dimensions — its pixel width and height. A photo resized from 4000 × 3000 to 1600 × 1200 literally has fewer pixels: about 1.9 million instead of 12 million. The picture's content is the same, rendered on a smaller grid.

Compressing changes how efficiently those pixels are stored, without changing their count. The image stays 4000 × 3000, but the file describing it gets smaller because the encoder records the pixels more cleverly — losslessly, by removing redundancy, or lossily, by approximating detail the eye will not miss, as explained in our guide to how compression works.

One changes how much information the image has; the other changes how much room that information takes up. File size depends on both, which is why the two are so often confused — and why using them together is so effective.

Why resizing is usually the bigger lever

Pixel counts multiply. Halving an image's width and height does not halve the data — it quarters it, because area shrinks in both directions at once. A 4000-pixel-wide photo carries roughly six times the pixels of a 1600-pixel-wide version of the same shot. Compression, by contrast, typically saves 50–80% on a photograph — substantial, but bounded by how much redundancy and invisible detail the image contains. When an image is far larger than its destination, resizing wins by a landslide; when the dimensions are already right, compression is the only lever left. Knowing your image's destination size is therefore the master skill — it is also why resolution questions are really questions about matching pixels to purpose.

Which do you need? A decision guide

  • The image is bigger in pixels than it will ever be displayed. Resize first — this is most camera photos headed for the web, email or documents. A 4000 px photo in an 800 px slot is carrying about 25 times the pixels the slot can show.
  • The dimensions are right but the file is still heavy. Compress. A correctly sized photo at quality 100, or saved as PNG, shrinks dramatically with no change in dimensions.
  • Both are wrong — the common case. Resize to the destination size, then compress the result. This is the standard workflow in our website compression guide, and the combination routinely removes 90% or more of the original file weight.
  • The image must keep exact dimensions (a form demands 600 × 600 px, a template is fixed). Resize to exactly that, then use quality alone to meet any file-size limit.
  • It is a screenshot, logo or graphic. Resize if oversized, but compress losslessly (PNG) or not at all — lossy compression is what blurs text, as our screenshot guide explains.

Order matters: resize first, then compress

Always resize before compressing, for two reasons. First, efficiency: there is no point spending computation precisely encoding millions of pixels you are about to throw away. Second, quality: a lossy encoder working on the full-size image makes its approximations at the wrong scale; resizing the already-compressed result then resamples those artefacts into the final image. Resize-then-compress gives the encoder the actual final pixels to optimise. In the compressor this ordering is automatic — set the maximum width or height and the quality together, and the tool scales first and encodes second.

Two warnings that apply to both

Never enlarge. Resizing up invents pixels by interpolation; it makes files bigger and pictures softer, and no setting reverses it. If an image is smaller than its destination, the honest fixes are a smaller display size or a better original.

Work on copies. Both operations are one-way doors — discarded pixels and discarded detail do not come back. Keep the original untouched, and produce resized, compressed copies for each purpose: one for the website, one for email, one for the archive. The rest of our guides are largely about choosing the right copy for each destination — now you know the two operations every one of those recipes is built from.

The one-line summary: resize to change how big the image is, compress to change how much it weighs — and for almost every real photo, do the first, then the second.
